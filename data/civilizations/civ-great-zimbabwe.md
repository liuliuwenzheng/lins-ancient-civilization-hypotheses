## Claims (EN)

- (fact) UNESCO and scholarly syntheses frame Great Zimbabwe as a major dry-stone capital and Zimbabwe Culture centre of the southern African Iron Age (UNESCO 364; Pikirayi 2001).
- (fact) Caton-Thompson 1931 excavation monograph established a foundational field case for Great Zimbabwe as a local African building tradition (Clarendon).
- (fact) Literature often compares successor stone capitals such as Khami with Great Zimbabwe (Pikirayi 2001; Huffman 2007).
- (fact) Garlake 1973 provides an accessible synthesis of Great Zimbabwe dry-stone architecture and history (Thames and Hudson).
- (fact) The Great Enclosure is framed by UNESCO and syntheses as hallmark freestanding dry-stone architecture (UNESCO 364; Garlake 1973).
- (fact) Hill Complex and Valley Ruins form core sectors of Great Zimbabwe in UNESCO and synthesis descriptions (UNESCO 364; Pikirayi 2001).
- (fact) Huffman 2007 Iron Age handbook places Zimbabwe Culture in southern African farming-society frames (UKZN Press); models are contested.
- (fact) Mapungubwe often appears in Zimbabwe Culture origins and early-state debates (Pikirayi 2001; Huffman 2007); relational models remain open.
- (fact) Ndoro 2001 discusses Great Zimbabwe as local shrine and heritage-management subject (Uppsala).
- (fact) Pikirayi 2001 synthesizes Zimbabwe Culture origins and decline of southern Zambezian states (AltaMira).
- (fact) UNESCO lists Great Zimbabwe National Monument as World Heritage, emphasizing dry-stone capital landscape (UNESCO 364).
